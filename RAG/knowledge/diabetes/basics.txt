Diabetes is a long-term condition in which blood glucose stays higher than the body can use safely. Glucose comes from food and is carried in the blood to provide energy. Insulin helps move glucose from the blood into cells. In type 2 diabetes, the body may not use insulin well and may gradually make less of it. Type 1 diabetes happens when the immune system destroys the cells that make insulin.

Over time, high blood glucose can affect the heart and blood vessels, eyes, kidneys, nerves, and feet. Many people do not notice symptoms when glucose is above their usual range, so regular follow-up and monitoring are important even when someone feels well. Possible symptoms of high glucose include increased thirst, frequent urination, tiredness, and blurred vision.

Diabetes care usually combines healthy eating patterns, regular physical activity, monitoring, medicines when prescribed, and routine health checks. The right plan depends on the type of diabetes, other health conditions, age, preferences, and treatment goals. A clinician can help interpret readings and adjust the plan safely.

Low blood glucose can cause sweating, shakiness, hunger, confusion, dizziness, or unusual behavior, especially in people using insulin or some glucose-lowering medicines. Severe or worsening symptoms need prompt help. Patients should learn which symptoms matter for them and discuss an individual response plan with their healthcare team.
